What is PiP?

PiP (Percentile-in-Percentile) measures research impact on a 0-to-1 scale by comparing like with like at both the paper level and the author level:

  1. Paper citation percentile: Each cited work is assigned its citation percentile among cited works published in the same year, putting older and newer papers on equal footing.
  2. Peer standing at each threshold: At each citation-percentile threshold θ (in 40 steps of 2.5 percentile points), we count how many works the author has at or above θ and compute g(θ), the share of active peers who started publishing in the same year and have strictly fewer such works.
  3. Averaging above the cutoff (τ = 0.5): The PiP score is the average height of the standing curve g(θ) across thresholds at or above the 50th percentile (τ = 0.5). Only works in the top half of their publication year earn credit, so uncited or low-cited entries cannot pad the score.

On an author's profile, you can adjust the cutoff τ on the Standing curve chart to see how shifting τ moves the score between publication quantity (lower τ) and top-end impact (higher τ). Cohort and field rankings use the standard τ = 0.50 cutoff. The explorer also includes a By publication year scatter view and the earlier PiP-AUC curve view for comparison.

What is PiP-AUC?

PiP-AUC (Percentile-in-Percentile Area Under Curve) combines citation quality with publication productivity into a single 0-to-1 score. Each paper is ranked against all papers published in the same year, and an author's publication count is compared to faculty at similar career stages.

{% endif %}

How are percentiles calculated?

All metrics ({% if data_source == "gs" %}PiP{% else %}PiP-AUC{% endif %}, h-index, citations, i10-index, cited publication count) are percentile-ranked {% if data_source == "gs" %} against active authors among the Google Scholar profiles we track {% else %} against active authors in the Semantic Scholar dataset {% endif %} who started publishing in the same year. This age-aware normalization prevents bias toward senior researchers.

How does the field comparison work?

Fields differ in how much people publish and how often they cite, so each author also has a field page, linked from the results page, that ranks the PiP score and the four conventional metrics among active authors in one field whose career started within a few years of the author's (the window widens until it holds at least 100 authors). It starts on the author's field, taken from their Google Scholar interests or, when those name no field, from the interests of their co-authors. People who work across fields can pick any of the others, and the link keeps that choice. There are 11 fields: Computer Science; Mathematics & Statistics; Physics & Astronomy; Chemistry & Materials; Engineering; Earth & Environmental Sciences; Biology & Neuroscience; Medicine & Health; Psychology & Cognitive Science; Economics & Business; Social Sciences & Humanities.

How often is data updated?

{% if data_source == "gs" %} Author and publication data comes from public Google Scholar profiles. Profiles are refreshed on a rolling schedule, and a profile we have not seen before is fetched the first time it is requested. Citation-over-time charts are not available yet. {% else %} Author and publication data is sourced from the Semantic Scholar Academic Graph Dataset, which is updated weekly. Distribution tables (population percentiles) are refreshed quarterly. {% endif %}
