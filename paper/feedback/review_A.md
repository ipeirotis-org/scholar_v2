# Peer Review Report for *Quantitative Science Studies* (QSS)

**Manuscript Title:** Percentile in Percentile: rating scholars against their career cohort by the impact of all their papers  
**Authors:** Sen Tian and Panos Ipeirotis  
**Target Journal:** [Quantitative Science Studies (MIT Press / ISSI)](https://direct.mit.edu/qss/pages/submission-guidelines)  
**Recommendation:** **Major Revision** (Strong potential contribution; requires methodological sensitivity checks and alignment with field-normalization standards in scientometrics)

## 1. Summary and Overall Assessment

This manuscript introduces **Percentile in Percentile ($\text{PiP}_\tau$)**, an author-level bibliometric indicator that normalizes twice: first placing each of a scholar's articles into a citation percentile $y_j(Y) \in [0, 1)$ within its publication year $p_j$ as observed at evaluation year $Y$, and then, for every quality threshold $\theta \in [\tau, 1]$, ranking the scholar's count of articles $n_a(\theta)$ at or above $\theta$ against active peers in the same career cohort (authors whose first article appeared in the same start year $s_a$). Averaging this cohort standing $g_a(\theta)$ over $\theta \ge \tau$ (with default $\tau = 0.5$) yields a bounded score in $[0, 1)$.

The paper substantially improves upon both the authors' earlier sum-of-percentiles preprint ([Tian & Ipeirotis, 2021](https://arxiv.org/abs/2103.16025)) and the asymmetric $\text{PiP-AUC}$ formulation (Ipeirotis, 2024), which compared $n_a(\theta)$ against peers' *total* article counts $n_b(0)$ rather than their thresholded counts $n_b(\theta)$—a flaw that assigned zero weight to an active author's top two papers and caused cohort drift when paired with a quality cutoff. Evaluated on 11.1 million disambiguated authors (111.6 million author-age observations) from the Semantic Scholar Academic Graph using strictly point-in-time citation windows (no look-ahead bias), $\text{PiP}_{0.5}$ exhibits impressive cohort stability from 1960 to 2015, traces a clear quantity–quality frontier as $\tau$ varies, predicts future 10-year output (years $Y+1$ to $Y+10$) without mechanical overlap, detects future "stars" roughly 1.8–1.9 years earlier than the $h$-index, and anticipates later academy memberships and scientific honors in Wikidata.

**Fit for *Quantitative Science Studies*:** The topic, empirical scale, open replication code (SQL/BigQuery scripts), and transparent treatment of limitations (such as cumulative advantage and [Waltman & van Eck's 2012](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/The-inconsistency-of-the-h-index-Waltman-Eck/66dfb34480d7c5f4b204c744aef6a0b9c4512eef) inconsistency property) align closely with QSS's mission and open-science standards. However, several methodological choices—most notably the discrete 40-point grid that truncates discrimination above the 97.5th percentile, the asymmetric treatment of ties via strict inequalities, the citation-dependent activity filter ($h \ge 3$), and treating field normalization as a secondary extension rather than the baseline—will draw immediate scrutiny from the QSS readership. Addressing these issues will make the paper a standout contribution to the scientometric indicators literature.

## 2. Major Comments

### 2.1 Discretization Truncation Above the 97.5th Percentile (Theory vs. Computation Mismatch)
In Sections 1, 4.3, and 4.4, the manuscript repeatedly emphasizes that a single top-1% paper earns credit at the highest thresholds (e.g., lines 57–58: *"a single paper in the top 1% earns credit at every threshold, including the highest ones, where most peers have nothing"*; lines 174–176: *"A single article in the top 1% raises $n_a(\theta)$ by one at every threshold, including the highest... a record of one article at the 99th percentile scores 0.45"*).

However, Section 4.6 (line 212) states that the integral is evaluated using a left Riemann sum on a 40-point grid:
$$\theta_k = \frac{k}{40}, \quad k = 0, \dots, 39 \implies \text{PiP}_{1/2}(a) = \frac{1}{20} \sum_{k=20}^{39} g_a(\theta_k)$$
Because the highest grid point is $\theta_{39} = 39/40 = 0.975$, **any article with $y_j(Y) \ge 0.975$ (top 2.5%) is treated identically in the empirical computation**. An article at the 97.6th percentile and a landmark article at the 99.9th percentile increment $n_a(\theta_k)$ at the exact same set of grid points ($k = 0, \dots, 39$). Furthermore, when testing $\tau = 0.9$ in Section 5.2 (Table 2, Figure 3), the score averages over only four grid thresholds ($0.90, 0.925, 0.95, 0.975$).
- **Actionable Recommendation:** Either (a) refine the upper tail of the grid (e.g., adding $\theta \in \{0.98, 0.99, 0.995, 0.999\}$ with appropriate quadrature weights $\Delta\theta_k$ so that top-1% and top-0.1% papers are actually differentiated as claimed in the text), or (b) explicitly reconcile lines 57 and 174–176 with Section 4.6 and report a sensitivity check comparing the 40-point uniform grid against a finer or tail-refined grid.

### 2.2 Tie-Breaking Rules (`<` vs. Mid-Ranks) and Cohort Drift at High Thresholds
Equations (1) and (3) define both the paper percentile $y_j(Y)$ and the cohort standing $g_a(\theta)$ using *strict* inequalities ($c_i(Y) < c_j(Y)$ and $n_b(\theta) < m$), whereas Section 5.1 (line 219) switches to Hazen mid-ranks for paper-level correlations, and Equation (5) (line 178) switches to a *weak* inequality ($n_b(0) \le m$) for the productivity percentile $F(m)$ so that $F(n_a(0)) > 0$.
- First, this creates an internal discrepancy in the factorization $\text{PiP}_\tau(a) = F(n_a(0)) \cdot Q_\tau(a)$ (Eq. 5): at $\theta = 0$, the integrand numerator is $g_a(0) = G_0(n_a(0))$ (strict inequality $< n_a(0)$), whereas the denominator is $F(n_a(0))$ (weak inequality $\le n_a(0)$). For an author with the minimum active article count $n_a(0) = 3$, $g_a(0) = 0$ even though $F(3) > 0$.
- Second, as shown by [Waltman & Schreiber (2013)](https://arxiv.org/abs/1205.0646) and Bornmann & Williams (2020), strict-inequality percentile rules distort distributions when ties are prevalent. At high quality thresholds $\theta$ and early career ages, a large fraction $p_0(\theta)$ of peers tie at $n_b(\theta) = 0$. Under strict inequality, all tied authors at zero receive $G_\theta(0) = 0$, while an author with a single paper ($n_a(\theta) = 1$) jumps immediately to $G_\theta(1) = p_0(\theta)$. Because average publication volume per author has risen over time, $p_0(\theta)$ shrinks across cohorts from 1960 to 2015—which is the primary mechanical driver of why $\text{PiP}_\tau$ drifts for $\tau > 0.6$ (lines 244–246, 272–273) and why the 25th percentile of $\text{PiP}_{0.5}$ drifts downward from 0.20 to 0.16 (line 268).
- **Actionable Recommendation:** Discuss how fractional tie-breaking / mid-ranks ($G_\theta^{\text{mid}}(m) = \frac{|\{b: n_b(\theta) < m\}| + 0.5|\{b: n_b(\theta) = m\}|}{|\mathcal{P}|}$) or the [Waltman & Schreiber (2013)](https://arxiv.org/abs/1205.0646) fractional percentile rule affect cohort stability at $\tau > 0.6$ and the factorization in Equation (5).

### 2.3 Field Normalization Should Be Foregrounded and Refined (Section 5.8)
In scientometrics, pooling all disciplines into a single citation reference set is universally recognized as invalid for cross-field evaluation (Hicks et al., 2015; Waltman, 2016). Indeed, Figure 8 demonstrates that pooled $\text{PiP}_{0.5}$ places 14.7% of physicists into the top 10% of their cohort compared to just 1.4% of education researchers and 2.1% of economists. While Section 5.8 introduces a field-normalized version, two issues need attention:
1. **Hybrid Paper-Field Rule (lines 413–418):** In the field-normalized version, *"a paper's percentile is computed within its publication year and the author's main field (when the paper carries that field; otherwise the pooled percentile is kept)."* Why fall back to the *unnormalized pooled percentile* when an author publishes outside their primary field? If an economist or mathematician co-authors an interdisciplinary paper tagged in Medicine or Biology, falling back to the pooled percentile artificially inflates that paper's standing relative to their main-field peers. Computing every paper's percentile within its own assigned field(s) (or averaging across its assigned fields) before aggregating at the author level would eliminate the residual inter-field spread (6.9% to 11.6% in Figure 8).
2. **Granularity of Semantic Scholar's 23 Fields:** Semantic Scholar's 23 top-level fields (e.g., Medicine, Biology, Computer Science, Engineering) aggregate subfields with vastly different citation densities and half-lives. The manuscript should discuss how coarse field definitions affect within-field bias and, ideally, test whether finer subfield normalization (e.g., OpenAlex concepts/subfields or MeSH/arXiv categories where available) alters the results.

### 2.4 Endogenous Selection Bias in the "Active Author" Filter ($n \ge 3$ and $h \ge 3$)
Section 3 (lines 135–138) defines an author as *active* at evaluation year $Y$ if they have at least 3 articles and an $h$-index of at least 3 through year $Y$. Conditioning peer group membership $\mathcal{P}$ on $h \ge 3$ introduces a **citation- and field-dependent truncation**:
- At career age 3 or 5, requiring $h \ge 3$ (at least 3 papers with $\ge 3$ citations each) filters out a much larger share of the cohort in slow-citing or low-volume fields (humanities, law, mathematics, economics) and in earlier historical eras (1960s–1970s, when citation rates were 40% lower) than in biomedical fields or the 2010s.
- Moreover, using $h \ge 3$ to define the peer group when benchmarking PiP against the $h$-index compresses the lower tail of the $h$-index distribution at early career ages (as noted on line 283: *"partly because many authors share the values 3 and 4"*), mechanically penalizing the $h$-index's rank correlation in early-career settings.
- **Actionable Recommendation:** Report a sensitivity check where active authors are defined purely by publication activity (e.g., $n_a(0) \ge 3$ or $\ge 5$ articles, without the $h \ge 3$ citation hurdle) to verify that PiP's early-detection and age-5 predictive advantages over the $h$-index are not amplified by truncating the cohort at $h = 3$.

### 2.5 Co-Authorship, Fractional Counting, and Reference Set Document Types
1. **Whole vs. Fractional Counting:** Under whole counting, a researcher in a large experimental collaboration (e.g., ATLAS/CMS in physics or large medical consortia) who is listed on 30 papers a year receives full credit for every paper above $\tau = 0.5$. Since the SQL pipeline is already built in BigQuery, testing fractional counting—either dividing paper credit by author count $1/k_j$ in $n_a(\theta)$ as recommended by Waltman & van Eck (2015), or reporting results stratified by median co-author count—would substantially strengthen the paper's practical relevance for hiring and promotion committees.
2. **Pooling Review Articles with Original Research (lines 127–129):** The reference set pools journal articles, conference papers, and *reviews* (`reviews, studies, clinical trials, meta-analyses`). Because review articles and meta-analyses systematically attract 2–3 times as many citations as regular articles, pooling them inflates the percentiles of review authors. Please clarify what fraction of the 71.5M articles are reviews/meta-analyses and whether normalizing reviews separately affects the indicator.

## 3. Minor Comments and Textual Corrections

1. **Mismatch in Number of Contributions (pages 2–3, lines 62–81):** Line 62 states *"The paper makes four contributions:"*, but the enumerated list immediately following it contains **six** items (1. Definition and properties, 2. Measurement over time, 3. Prediction, 4. Early detection, 5. Later recognition, 6. Robustness). Please update line 62 to *"six contributions"* (or consolidate items 3–5).
2. **Clarification on `https://www.pip-score.org` (lines 87, 515, 522):** Line 87 states that the first, asymmetric form ($\text{PiP-AUC}$) *"is used by the website https://www.pip-score.org"*, and line 523 repeats that it *"computes the original PiP-AUC from Google Scholar profiles."* However, the Conclusion (lines 514–515) implies that a public implementation of the paper's proposed PiP indicator is available at that URL. Please clarify whether the website has been updated to display the new symmetric $\text{PiP}_{0.5}$ alongside or in place of $\text{PiP-AUC}$.
3. **Outcome Scoring Window at the End of the Dataset (Section 5.5, line 286–287):** Future articles published in years $Y+1$ to $Y+10$ are scored *"each at its own age 5, within its publication year."* Since citation data end in calendar year 2025 (line 124), an evaluation year of $Y = 2005$ uses future papers up to $2015$, which reach age 5 by $2020 \le 2025$. However, for the "Same career age" setting (line 292: *"from every start year since 1975"*), please explicitly state the last start year / evaluation year $Y$ included so that all $Y+10$ future papers have a full 5-year citation window by 2025 (presumably $Y \le 2010$, i.e., start year $\le 2000$ for career age 10, as noted in Table 2).
4. **Definition of "Career Age" and Career Interruptions (Section 3, line 133–134):** Defining $s_a$ as the year of the very first article means that an author who published one undergraduate paper in 2000, finished a PhD in 2008, and became an assistant professor in 2009 is evaluated in 2014 as "career age 14" rather than 6 years post-PhD. While noted briefly in Section 6 (line 491), have the authors tested defining $s_a$ as the year of the *second* or *third* paper (or first year with active publishing) to reduce sensitivity to isolated pre-doctoral publications?
5. **Author Contributions Section (page 22, line 527):** The heading `Author contributions` on page 22 is currently blank. QSS requires a CRediT (Contributor Roles Taxonomy) author statement before publication.
6. **Appendix B Formatting (pages 25–26, line 613):** The heading `B Prediction results in all settings` appears at the very bottom of page 25 as an orphan heading, while Table 8 floats to the middle of page 26 with no accompanying text.
